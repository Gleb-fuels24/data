Method

How the reading is produced

Pull the imagery

A scheduled job requests the latest Sentinel-2 scene over each zone of interest from the Copernicus archive and crops it to the trains, the storage area and the berths.

Find the heat

Working equipment and flares are far brighter in short-wave infrared than their surroundings. The classifier compares band 12 against bands 11 and 8 and counts hot pixels inside each zone.

Confirm against a second source

VIIRS thermal readings are checked independently. When the image says hot and VIIRS agrees, confidence is high; when they disagree, the zone is marked no data rather than guessed.

Watch the ships

The vessel watcher listens to AIS, keeps tankers over 230 m moving under 0.6 knots inside a berth polygon, and counts a call as a loading only after eight hours alongside.

Have a human sign it off

An analyst reads the chips before anything is published. Automation finds candidates; it does not get the last word.

What this method cannot do, stated plainly. Sentinel-2 returns to the same ground about every 5 days, and cloud can cost a pass, so a status here can be up to a week old — the timestamp above is the real one. Thermal signature shows that equipment is hot, not how much LNG left the site. And a berth maps to a joint venture, never to an individual train: output from all trains flows into shared storage before loading, so anyone who claims to tie one cargo to one train is guessing. We would rather publish a dated, bounded reading than a confident one.
